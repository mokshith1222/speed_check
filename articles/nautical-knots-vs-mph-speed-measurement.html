Nautical Knots vs MPH vs KM/H: History, Mathematics, and Applications

Why do ships and commercial aircraft measure velocity in "knots" instead of statute miles per hour or kilometers per hour? Discover the spherical Earth geometry and navigational history behind the knot.

1. The 16th-Century Origin: The Chip Log and Knotted Rope

Before modern satellite navigation or electronic pitot tubes, 16th-century mariners required a reliable method to calculate vessel speed across open oceans. They devised a device called the Chip Log: a pie-shaped wooden board weighted with lead along one curved edge so it would float vertically in the water and resist being pulled forward.

The chip was fastened to a long spool of light rope, which had physical knots tied into it at regular intervals (typically 47 feet and 3 inches apart).

When thrown overboard from the stern of a moving sailing vessel, the line paid out freely. A sailor inverted a 28-second sand hourglass while another sailor counted the number of physical knots that slipped through their hands before the sand ran out.

If 6 knots passed through the sailor's fingers in 28 seconds, the ship was traveling at "6 knots".

2. Mathematical Definition of the Nautical Mile

Unlike the arbitrary statute mile (defined historically as 5,280 Roman paces) or the metric kilometer (originally defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole), the Nautical Mile (NM) is derived directly from the geometry of the Earth.

The Nautical Definition: One nautical mile is equal to exactly one minute of arc of latitude ($1'$) along any meridian of longitude.

Because there are $360^\circ$ in a circle and $60\text{ arcminutes}$ in each degree:

$$\text{Earth Circumference} = 360^\circ \times 60' = 21,600\text{ Nautical Miles}$$

In 1929, the First International Extraordinary Hydrographic Conference in Monaco established the international standard:

$$1\text{ International Nautical Mile} = 1,852\text{ meters exactly } (6,076.12\text{ feet})$$

Therefore, 1 Knot (kn) = 1 Nautical Mile per Hour:

$$1\text{ Knot} = 1.852\text{ km/h} = 1.15078\text{ mph} = 0.51444\text{ m/s}$$

3. Why Aviation & Maritime Navigation Exclusively Use Knots

In an era of advanced GPS avionics, why do Boeing, Airbus, the US Navy, and international air traffic control retain knots?

The answer lies in seamless chart plotting. When an aircraft pilot or ship captain cruises at 120 knots for 30 minutes, they have traveled exactly 60 nautical miles. On a standard navigational chart, 60 nautical miles corresponds directly to 1 full degree of latitude ($1^\circ$).

Navigators can immediately place dividers on the latitude scale of a paper chart or electronic flight bag (EFB) to measure distance without performing tedious unit conversions between statute miles and coordinates.

5. Airspeed vs Ground Speed via GPS Telematics

In aviation, there is a fundamental distinction between Indicated Airspeed (IAS) measured by forward-facing pitot tubes (which senses dynamic air pressure $\frac{1}{2}\rho v^2$) and Ground Speed (GS) measured over the Earth's surface.

If an aircraft flies with an airspeed of 150 knots directly into a 40-knot headwind, its actual speed relative to ground tracking stations is only 110 knots.
