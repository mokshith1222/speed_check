Vehicle Speed vs Fuel Economy: The Drag Equation and Cruising Sweet Spots

Why does driving 120 km/h consume disproportionately more gasoline and battery charge than cruising at 90 km/h? Analyze fluid dynamics, rolling resistance friction, and engine efficiency maps.

1. The Non-Linear Cost of Speed

Most drivers understand intuitively that driving faster burns more fuel. However, few motorists appreciate just how steep and unforgiving the underlying physical penalty curve is. Increasing your cruising speed from 100 km/h (62 mph) to 130 km/h (81 mph) represents a 30% increase in speed, but it typically demands 45% to 65% more engine power and increases total fuel consumption by 25% to 35% per kilometer.

To understand why this happens, we must examine the fundamental forces opposing vehicle motion on a roadway.

2. Total Road Load Equation

When a vehicle moves at constant velocity along a flat, paved highway, the tractive effort $F_{\text{traction}}$ required from the engine or electric motor equals the sum of two dominant resistive forces:

$$F_{\text{total}} = F_{\text{rolling}} + F_{\text{aerodynamic}}$$

A. Rolling Resistance ($F_{\text{rolling}}$)

Rolling resistance is caused by the mechanical hysteresis and deformation of tire elastomeric compounds as they rotate against asphalt:

$$F_{\text{rolling}} = C_{rr} \cdot m \cdot g$$

Where $C_{rr}$ is the coefficient of rolling resistance ($\approx 0.008\text{ to } 0.012$ for modern low-rolling-resistance tires), $m$ is vehicle mass ($kg$), and $g$ is gravitational acceleration ($9.81\text{ m/s}^2$). Crucially, rolling resistance is roughly linear with respect to velocity.

B. Aerodynamic Drag ($F_{\text{aerodynamic}}$)

As speed increases, aerodynamic resistance quickly overwhelms rolling resistance according to the classical fluid dynamics drag equation:

$$F_{\text{aero}} = \frac{1}{2} \rho \cdot v^2 \cdot C_d \cdot A$$

Where:

  • $\rho$ is ambient air density ($\approx 1.225\text{ kg/m}^3$ at sea level at 15°C).
  • $v$ is vehicle velocity relative to the air mass ($m/s$).
  • $C_d$ is the aerodynamic drag coefficient ($\approx 0.23$ for streamlined sedans; $\approx 0.38$ for large SUVs).
  • $A$ is the frontal projected surface area of the vehicle ($m^2$, typically $2.1 - 2.8\text{ m}^2$).

3. The Cubic Power Law: Why 130 km/h Punishes Your Wallet

While the drag force increases quadratically ($v^2$), the mechanical power required to overcome that drag at a given velocity is force multiplied by speed ($P = F \cdot v$), leading to a cubic relationship:

$$P_{\text{aero}} = \frac{1}{2} \rho \cdot v^3 \cdot C_d \cdot A$$

Observe the dramatic power escalation required just to punch through air resistance on a standard midsize crossover ($C_d = 0.32$, $A = 2.4\text{ m}^2$):

4. The Engine Sweet Spot: Brake Specific Fuel Consumption (BSFC)

If aerodynamic resistance is lowest at 20 km/h, why isn't crawling at 20 km/h the most fuel-efficient cruising speed?

Internal combustion engines (ICE) suffer from thermal, mechanical, and pumping losses at very low RPM and low engine loads. An automotive engine reaches its peak thermal efficiency (lowest Brake Specific Fuel Consumption or BSFC) in its highest overdrive transmission gear at approximately 1,500 to 2,000 RPM.

When you balance thermodynamic engine efficiency against rising aerodynamic drag, the universal optimal cruising speed for internal combustion passenger cars is between 75 and 90 km/h (47 to 56 mph).

For Electric Vehicles (EVs), which maintain ~90% motor efficiency across almost all RPM ranges, energy consumption per kilometer rises monotonically with speed. (See our companion guide: EV Speed vs Range Analysis).

5. Practical Time vs Cost Calculations for Road Trips

Consider a typical 200 km (124 mile) interstate highway journey:

  • Cruising at 100 km/h: Trip time = 120.0 minutes. Fuel consumed = ~13.0 Liters ($22.10 at $1.70/L).
  • Cruising at 130 km/h: Trip time = 92.3 minutes. Fuel consumed = ~17.5 Liters ($29.75 at $1.70/L).

Speeding saved only 27 minutes, but burned an extra 4.5 liters of fuel, increased brake and tire wear, elevated collision risk exponentially, and incurred severe vulnerability to radar speed traps.
